# Plain language summary

## Fabricated citations from large language models: how often, how badly, and which models

Ask a chatbot for references for a medical topic and it will hand you a tidy list. Some of those papers do not exist. The authors sound right, the journal is real, the year fits, and there is no such article. Other references point at a real paper but get the title, authors or DOI wrong badly enough that a reader cannot find the source. This review collected every study it could find in which researchers asked a large language model for biomedical references and then checked each reference against PubMed, Crossref, Google Scholar or a publisher record. It pooled the results.

Twenty five experiments met the rules, covering 69 model setups. In the studies that counted completely fabricated references, about 19 in every 100 generated references did not exist at all (pooled 18.6 percent, 95 percent confidence interval 12.8 to 26.3 percent, 51 model setups, 12,828 references checked). In the studies that counted references with no serious error, about 29 in every 100 were fully accurate on every field checked (pooled 29.4 percent, 19.5 to 41.7 percent, 42 setups, 12,002 references). The rest sit in between: real papers with wrong details. A smaller group of five studies counted major inaccuracies including fabrication, and pooled 36.0 percent (19.8 to 56.3 percent).

The averages hide a wide spread, and the spread is the finding. One radiology study generated 480 references from each of six models and found fabrication from 3 percent (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, 15 of 480) to 61 percent (Gemini 1.5 Pro, 291 of 480). Models with web access turned on fabricated far less in the studies that tested them: pooled 5.0 percent with retrieval against 25.1 percent with retrieval off, though only five retrieval setups were available, so that gap is a signal, not a settled result. A separate audit of 2.5 million published papers, kept apart from the experiments in this review, found fabricated citations in print rising from about 1 in 2,828 papers in 2023 to about 1 in 277 in early 2026. Errors made in experiments are reaching journals.

How sure are we? Not very. The methods work was rated very low certainty for all three main outcomes. Studies disagreed with each other far more than chance explains, many never said who checked the references or whether the checker was blind to which model wrote them, and 15 of the 25 studies sat at high risk of bias on a tool built for this kind of experiment. The honest reading is that fabrication is common, varies a lot by model and setup, and has not been measured as carefully as the claims made about it. Anyone who uses a model to draft a reference list should check every reference against a database before the list goes anywhere. That is a research integrity point, not medical advice, and this review makes no recommendation about patient care.
